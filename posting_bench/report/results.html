What docID clustering buys

The uniform collection has exactly the same list lengths as the clustered one, but random docIDs. Comparing the two isolates the effect of docID order, which real systems control by reordering documents (by URL, or by recursive graph bisection).

  • Codecs that model local density gain the most. On the query lists, clustering cuts Run-Trie by 50%, Trie by 46%, interpolative by 42%, PEF by 39%, OptPFor by 33% and Roaring by 32%. BP128 gains 18% and VByte only 12%, because one wide gap still forces a wide block or extra bytes.
  • Plain Elias-Fano gains nothing (8.08 → 8.13 bits): its size depends only on \(u\) and \(n\), exactly as the formula says. On uniform data it ties PEF as the smallest codec, which is the case partitioning cannot improve.
  • Roaring and the tries pay most for randomness. At 13.2–13.7 bits per posting on uniform data they are 1.6–1.7× the size of PEF, because random docIDs leave few runs and many half-empty 16-bit arrays or trie paths.
  • Conjunctions get about 2× faster with clustering for every list codec (PEF 0.083 → 0.043 ms, BP128 0.156 → 0.076 ms), while Roaring changes little. Part of this comes from the workloads: the uniform queries touch 19% more postings and have smaller results, because random lists share fewer documents. The rest comes from skipping, since clustered lists put matching docIDs in fewer blocks and partitions.

Kernels: where merging stops paying

The pairwise kernels were timed on uncompressed random lists with a fixed short list of 10,000 docIDs and a long list 1 to 4,096 times larger. The chart plots nanoseconds per element of the short list, so a flat line means the cost tracks the short list (the \(m \log(n/m)\) regime) and a rising line means the long list is scanned (the \(m + n\) regime).

  • Similar sizes favour SIMD. At 1:1 the AVX-512 kernel takes 22 µs against 98 µs for the branchy merge, 4.4× faster; SSE and AVX2 take 25–26 µs, so most of the gain comes from comparing blocks at all, not from vector width. The branch-free merge (66 µs) beats the branchy one only at 1:1 and becomes the slowest kernel by 1:8, because its loop-carried dependency serialises every step.
  • Skew favours galloping. Galloping overtakes the SIMD kernel between 1:16 and 1:32 and is 5.8× faster at 1:4,096 (9.6 ms against 55 ms). At 1:4,096 Baeza-Yates is within 13% of galloping and plain binary search is 2.6× slower; both lose badly when the sizes are similar (binary search needs 389 µs at 1:1).
  • The switch point is hardware-specific. The adaptive kernel uses CRoaring's 64× threshold. On this CPU that is too late: between 1:32 and 1:64 it pays up to 2.1× more than galloping. A threshold near 1:24 would track the lower envelope of both kernels, consistent with Baeza-Yates and Salinger's ~30:1 crossover for scalar code.

Longer queries

Each point uses 300 queries with exactly \(k\) co-occurring terms. As \(k\) grows from 2 to 6, the lists in a query hold more postings in total (400k to 740k–880k), yet every codec answers faster: the mean result shrinks from 744 to 2 documents, and the cost of a rarest-first conjunction follows the shortest list and the number of candidates it proposes, not the total length.

  • Roaring's lead narrows with more terms. It is 1.9× faster than PEF for two-term queries (0.028 against 0.054 ms) but only 1.3× faster at six (0.011 against 0.014 ms). Selective conjunctions let cursor-based codecs take long NextGEQ jumps, while Roaring still visits every shared 2¹⁶ chunk, the effect Pibiri & Venturini describe.
  • PEF matches or beats uncompressed arrays from three terms on, at less than a sixth of the space, because a select jump over the high bits costs about as much as galloping over raw integers.
  • Tries stay 2.6–3.5× behind PEF at every length. The likely reason is that the traversal pays a rank operation per trie level for every surviving region, which costs more than one cursor jump when a single list is very selective.

Real text: WikiText-103

WikiText-103 has 859,532 paragraphs, a 531,033-term vocabulary and 57 million postings. Its lists are far denser than the synthetic ones: function words occur in most paragraphs. The queries use terms with at least 100 postings and average 3.0 terms, with 64 documents per AND and 199,635 per OR.

  • Dense real lists suit Roaring. Its bitmap containers answer AND in 0.007 ms, 2.7× faster than the fastest list codec (Elias-Fano, 0.019 ms), and OR in 0.086 ms, 10× faster than any list codec with a cursor algorithm (0.86–1.6 ms) and 4.5× faster than the hybrid index. On the query lists it costs 8.47 bits per posting, 1.8× PEF.
  • The Elias-Fano family leads the list codecs: PEF and interpolative coding are the smallest (4.75 and 4.77 bits per posting on query lists), and Elias-Fano and PEF answer conjunctions 1.4–4.3× faster than the block codecs.
  • Small lists dominate whole-index space. Over all 531k lists the ranking changes: interpolative (8.46), Elias-Fano (8.51) and PEF (8.92) stay smallest, but the tries reach 23 bits per posting, because each of the many short lists pays for about 20 trie levels and their rank directories. Roaring pays a container header per list and lands at 15.4.
  • The OR algorithm matters as much as the codec. For every cursor codec except the hybrid, the Lucene-style bitset window is the fastest union, 1.6–2.5× faster than the heap.
