# Representation, Not Algorithm, Now Drives Posting-List Speed

As of October 2026, fast intersection and union of compressed posting lists comes mostly from data layout and execution style, not from new set-operation algorithms. The intersection algorithm that wins in practice is still an old idea: let the rarest list lead and gallop or skip (NextGEQ) through the others. Twenty-five years of adaptive theory have produced tight bounds but no faster practical replacement ([Barbay et al. 2009](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~alopez-o/files/AEIoSIAfTS_2009.pdf)). Union speed is still set by how fast lists decode ([Pibiri & Venturini](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.10598)). The large gains come from representation. Universe-partitioned hybrids such as Roaring answer AND queries about **4–8× faster** and OR queries about **14–18× faster** than the best gap-compressed list codecs, but at roughly **twice the space** of partitioned Elias-Fano. Production engines now mix bit-packed blocks with bitset-encoded dense blocks (Lucene 10.2, 2025) ([Lucene CHANGES](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_4_0/changes/Changes.html)). SIMD adds a fairly stable **2–6×** for lists of similar size, much less for skewed lists and only about **1.5× for unions**. GPUs, processing-in-memory and ASICs mostly help batched throughput, graph mining and learned-sparse scoring, not single conjunctive queries. For ranked top-k retrieval, Lucene, Tantivy and iresearch all use the same pattern: block-max MaxScore or WAND chooses docID windows, and 4,096-document bitset/score-array accumulation runs inside each window. Learned sparse retrieval forced a further shift to docID-range block pruning and clustered approximate indexes, which cut SPLADE latency from hundreds of milliseconds to a few milliseconds or less. Compact tries and the learned codec LICO (SIGMOD 2026) report **1.5–5× faster** set operations than partitioned Elias-Fano, but neither produces smaller indexes, and both depend on reordered docIDs and long lists. The practical lesson running through all of this: docID reordering, plus a layout that lets an engine skip whole docID ranges and do word-parallel work inside the rest, matters more than which comparison-based algorithm it uses.

## Rarest-first galloping still beats asymptotically smarter algorithms

For two lists, the theory is settled. For sorted lists of sizes m ≤ n, any comparison-based intersection needs about log C(n+m, m) ≈ **m·log(n/m)** comparisons, a bound that goes back to Hwang and Lin (1972) ([Ding & König 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1103.2409)). Linear merge costs O(m+n). Galloping (doubling) search and Baeza-Yates' median-split recursion both reach the O(m log(n/m)) optimum, and both shift smoothly between merge-like and binary-search-like behavior ([Baeza-Yates & Salinger 2010](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~ajsaling/papers/intersection_alg_app10.pdf)). Which one wins in practice depends on constant factors, and the size ratio decides. Baeza-Yates and Salinger found merge faster until the ratio passes about **30:1**. Lemire, Boytsov and Kurz's SIMD heuristic switches from merge-like kernels to galloping between **50:1 and 1,000:1** ([Lemire et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.6399)). CRoaring hard-codes a **64×** threshold, marked "subject to tuning" ([CRoaring array.c](https://github.com/RoaringBitmap/CRoaring/blob/master/src/containers/array.c)).

For k lists, theory built a hierarchy of adaptive measures. Demaine, López-Ortiz and Munro (SODA 2000) defined instance difficulty through "proofs" (certificates). Their algorithm stays within 8kG comparisons, and they showed that a factor-k gap is unavoidable in their framework ([Demaine et al. 2000](https://erikdemaine.org/papers/SODA2000/paper.ps)). Barbay and Kenyon tightened this to **Θ(δ Σ log(n_i/δ))**, where the alternation δ is the size of the smallest certificate, and gave a deterministic algorithm that meets the bound ([Barbay & Kenyon 2008](https://users.dcc.uchile.cl/~jbarbay/Publications/2008-TALG-AlternationAndRedundancyAnalysisOfTheIntersectionProblem-BarbayKenyon.pdf)). The large experimental studies point the other way. On Google queries and TREC GOV2, the simplest method was fastest in CPU time: **SvS (set-versus-set, smallest list first) with galloping search**. The round-robin, certificate-chasing algorithms did poorly, because "most instances have a long certificate" ([Barbay et al. 2009](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~alopez-o/files/AEIoSIAfTS_2009.pdf)). Lemire et al. summarize the result: SvS with galloping was about **2× faster** than most alternatives on real GOV2 data, while Baeza-Yates' algorithm won by about 30% only on uniform synthetic data ([Lemire et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.6399)).

The main algorithmic challenger came from Ding and König's randomized hash-partitioned structures. They intersect k preprocessed sets in expected O(n/√w + kr) time and ran **8.4× faster** than δ-coded merge on Bing queries over Wikipedia. They also occupied 66% of uncompressed size, against 26–28% for compressed merge ([Ding & König 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1103.2409)). No production engine adopted them. Production code implements SvS directly. Lucene's `ConjunctionDISI` sorts sub-iterators by cost "to allow the sparser iterator to lead," leapfrogs the two cheapest, and turns dense bitset clauses into O(1) membership tests ([Lucene ConjunctionDISI](https://github.com/apache/lucene/blob/main/lucene/core/src/java/org/apache/lucene/search/ConjunctionDISI.java)). PISA's `and_query` sorts cursors by increasing frequency and probes the others with `next_geq` ([PISA and_query](https://github.com/pisa-engine/pisa/blob/master/include/pisa/query/algorithm/and_query.hpp)).

The same primitive sits inside worst-case-optimal joins. Their AGM-bound guarantees require each intersection to run in time bounded by its smallest input (the "min property"), which is exactly what galloping or leapfrog provides ([EmptyHeaded](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.02368); [Leapfrog Triejoin](https://arxiv.org/abs/1210.0481)). In the 2020s, the progress there has also been in systems and layout rather than comparisons. Free Join (SIGMOD 2023) merges hash tables and tries ([Free Join](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.10841)). HERO's hierarchical bitmap tree combined with graph reordering reports up to **188×** over merge intersection for triangle counting ([HERO, SIGMOD 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10533452)). An October 2025 paper shows that nested binary intersections over "seekable iterators" can be composed to match k-way leapfrog behavior ([Arntzenius 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26016v1)). New theory mostly confirms limits. van der Hoog, Rotenberg and Rutschmann (November 2025) proved that no deterministic comparison-based intersection algorithm can be o(log n)-competitive with the best algorithm on each structure class, and gave a matching O(log n) algorithm ([arXiv 2511.02954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02954)). 3SUM-based conditional lower bounds constrain preprocessed set intersection ([Kopelowitz, Pettie & Porat](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.6756); [Kopelowitz & Vassilevska Williams, ICALP 2020](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ICALP.2020.74)).

Union follows different rules, because its output is large. Demaine et al. gave an O(D) union-proof algorithm. They also showed that the union of read-only B-trees can be built in O(kG) time by sharing structure, so union need not cost Θ(output) when the output can reuse parts of the inputs ([Demaine et al. 2000](https://erikdemaine.org/papers/SODA2000/paper.ps)). Barbay and Kenyon extended adaptive analysis to t-threshold queries (Lucene's "minimum should match"), but no lower bound is known for t < k/2 ([Barbay & Kenyon, ISAAC 2003](https://www.academia.edu/48716831/Deterministic_Algorithm_for_the_t_Threshold_Set_Problem)). I found no 2020–2026 primary research on adaptive union. In practice, union is a heap-based k-way merge or windowed bitset accumulation. Pibiri and Venturini's survey states the key asymmetry for list codecs: intersection speed is "strictly correlated to that of NextGEQ," while union speed tracks sequential decoding ([Pibiri & Venturini](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.10598)). That one observation explains most of what follows. Intersection rewards layouts that can be skipped through. Union rewards fast decoding and word-parallel ORs. Top-k union gets its speed from pruning.

## Universe partitioning buys 4–18× speed for twice the space

Compressed posting lists come in two families, and they support set operations in different ways. **Cardinality-partitioned** codecs split a list into blocks of 128 or 256 postings, or into variable partitions, and gap-encode each one. Intersection then means skipping to a block and decoding it. **Universe-partitioned** layouts split the docID space instead. Roaring keys on the high 16 bits and stores each chunk as a sorted 16-bit array (up to 4,096 values), a 65,536-bit bitmap, or a run container ([Roaring library paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1709.07821v6)). Pibiri's Slicing works similarly. Because chunks line up across lists, intersection becomes a chunk-by-chunk bitmap AND or a 16-bit SIMD array intersection, with no per-element NextGEQ.

Pibiri and Venturini's benchmark (ACM CSUR 2021) remains the most complete head-to-head comparison. The table below gives representative figures. The source tables were partly garbled in text extraction, so treat the last digits with caution ([Pibiri & Venturini](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1908.10598)).

| Codec | Bits/int Gov2 | Bits/int CCNews | AND ms Gov2 | AND ms CCNews | OR ms Gov2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roaring | 6.63 | 9.49 | **0.59** | **2.45** | **3.66** |
| Slicing | 4.31 | 7.78 | 1.03 | 4.30 | 5.20 |
| QMX | 5.12 | 7.40 | 2.53 | 18.60 | 52.99 |
| VByte | 8.81 | 9.28 | 2.76 | 19.52 | 54.41 |
| PEF | 3.11 | 5.45 | 2.91 | 21.17 | 69.77 |
| DINT | 3.53 | 6.44 | 3.28 | 24.21 | — |
| Opt-PFor | 3.63 | 6.07 | 3.45 | 23.81 | — |
| BIC | **2.94** | **5.24** | 10.01 | 72.75 | — |

Roaring therefore buys about 4–8× faster AND and about 14–18× faster OR, at 1.7–2.1× the space of partitioned Elias-Fano (PEF). On ClueWeb09 and CCNews it is even larger than plain VByte. Pibiri's Slicing paper measured a still wider AND gap of **4–51×** over cardinality-partitioned indexes, depending on list density. When CRoaring was recompiled without SIMD, it performed the same as Slicing: "vectorization does the difference" ([Pibiri, On Slicing](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01032)). The AND gap narrows as queries get more terms, because selective conjunctions let list codecs take longer skips. The OR gap widens. Wang et al.'s 21-method study (SIGMOD 2017) independently found that Roaring has the fastest intersection of any compressed representation. However, it found SIMD-BP128-style list codecs fastest for union and decompression ([Wang et al. 2017](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/csjgwang/pubs/SIGMOD17-Bitmap.pdf)). The two studies disagree on union. The likely explanation is data (synthetic and database lists versus web postings) and implementation (heap-based multi-way list merging versus CRoaring's SIMD bitmap OR), but neither paper establishes this.

The Elias-Fano family is the space-efficient choice that still supports NextGEQ well. Vigna's quasi-succinct indexes store each element in at most **2 + ⌈log(u/n)⌉ bits**. NextGEQ is a `select0` on the high-bits array followed by a short scan, with no decompression of the list ([Vigna 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1206.4300); [Pibiri & Venturini](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1908.10598)). PEF encodes each variable-length partition as EF, as a bitvector, or not at all when the partition is a full run. A dynamic program picks a partitioning within (1+ε) of optimal. Under skip-heavy block-max WAND variants, PEF "almost matches" the fastest codecs while being more than 50% smaller than variable-byte indexes ([Mallia et al. 2019](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/daat-ecir19.pdf)). It also had the best random-access latency in the 2026 LICO benchmark ([Zhu et al. 2026](https://upenn.ferric.systems/dbscholar/sigmod_papers/2026/10.1145_3802079.pdf)). Its weakness is bit-level decoding at about 1 ns/int, which loses to SIMD bit-packing on OR and MaxScore workloads. Among block codecs, SIMD-BP128 is "nearly twice as fast" as varint-G8IU and PFOR while saving up to 2 bits per integer ([Lemire & Boytsov](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.2137)). It matches VByte query speed at about half the space on reordered indexes (5.42 vs 10.09 bits/docid on BP-ordered Gov2) ([Mallia et al. 2019](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/daat-ecir19.pdf)). Entropy coders (BIC, ANS, interpolative) produce the smallest indexes but are dominated on speed: BIC's AND is about 3.4× slower than PEF's. Skip pointers cost only **3–5% extra space** and "can improve the intersection performance dramatically" ([Wang et al. 2017](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/csjgwang/pubs/SIGMOD17-Bitmap.pdf)).

**Hybrid bitmap/list layouts** have been the consensus for nearly two decades. Moffat and Culpepper showed that storing "a small fraction" of lists as bitvectors has negligible effect on index size while speeding up queries ([ADCS 2007](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/ammoffat/abstracts/mc07adcs.html)). Their hybrid answered conjunctions faster than uncompressed arrays ([Culpepper & Moffat, TOIS 2010](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/ammoffat/abstracts/cm10acmtois.html)). Kane and Tompa added skips over 256-integer blocks and "semi-bitvectors" for the dense front of lists ([Kane thesis](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/bitstream/10012/8945/3/Kane_Andrew.pdf)). SIMD decoding and intersection then nearly doubled the hybrid's speed ([Lemire et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.6399)). The pattern keeps reappearing. LevelHeaded measured bitset∩bitset at about **50×** faster than uint∩uint for equal-cardinality sets ([LevelHeaded](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.07859)). Vespa keeps a bitvector alongside the posting list for frequent terms ([Vespa newsletter](https://blog.vespa.ai/vespa-newsletter-october-2022/)). Lucene 10.2 (April 2025) began encoding dense postings blocks directly as bitsets ([Lucene CHANGES](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_4_0/changes/Changes.html)).

**DocID reordering** is the lever that affects every representation. URL ordering shrinks a Packed+ANS2 index by 43% on Gov2, and randomly ordered indexes are "almost 3 times slower" to query ([Mallia et al. 2019](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/daat-ecir19.pdf)). Intersection over docID-sorted GOV2 runs 1.4–2× faster ([Lemire et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.6399)). Byte-aligned codecs gain little from reordering, so systems that apply recursive graph bisection (BP) should pair it with bit-level codecs.

Production choices differ from the research favorites. Lucene uses bit-packed FOR deltas without patching for docIDs, patched FOR for frequencies, and two levels of skip data inlined into the postings. It moved to **256-posting blocks in 10.4** ([Lucene 10.1 javadoc](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_1_0/core/org/apache/lucene/codecs/lucene101/Lucene101PostingsFormat.html); [Lucene CHANGES](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_4_0/changes/Changes.html)). Tantivy reimplements simdcomp-style 128-integer bit-packing ([tantivy_bitpacker](https://docs.rs/tantivy-bitpacker)). ClickHouse's 2026 text index stores postings as block-partitioned Roaring bitmaps with per-block min/max values for skipping ([ClickHouse blog](https://clickhouse.com/blog/clickhouse-full-text-search-object-storage)). None of the mainstream engines examined uses EF/PEF or entropy coding for postings. Where updates, decode speed and SIMD-friendliness dominate, bit-packing plus bitsets has won.

## SIMD delivers 2–6×, while GPUs and PIM chase throughput

CPU SIMD intersection matured between 2011 and 2020, and its lesson is that **cutting branches and comparisons matters more than vector width**. Schlegel et al. used SSE4.2 string-compare instructions to compare 8×8 blocks of 16-bit values all-against-all, reaching up to **5.3×** over scalar code ([Schlegel et al. 2011](http://www.adms-conf.org/p1-SCHLEGEL.pdf)). Lemire et al.'s V1/V3/SIMD-galloping family cut TREC GOV2 SvS query time from 2.8 ms (scalar) to 0.7 ms (galloping) to **0.5 ms (SIMD)** ([Lemire et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.6399)). Inoue et al.'s block merge cut branch mispredictions by more than 75%. It ran up to 5.2× faster than `std::set_intersection` and about 3× faster than V1 on similar-size arrays ([Inoue et al. 2014](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p293-inoue.pdf)). FESIA (ICDE 2020) prefilters with a hashed bitmap and finishes with specialized kernels in O(n/√w + r) time. It reports up to 7.6× over scalar and 1.4–3.5× over other SIMD methods, but its structure is built offline, taking 77.7 s for WebDocs ([FESIA](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~franzf/papers/icde2020_zhang.pdf)).

The most recent measurements come from a 2024 practitioner benchmark. On equal-size u16 sets, plain AVX-512BW ran *slower* than serial code (134 vs 222 thousand pairs/s at 1024/1024). A software emulation of VP2INTERSECT reached 1,338, about **6× serial** ([Vardanian 2024](https://ashvardanian.com/posts/simd-set-intersections-sve2-avx512/)). At 1:64 skew the picture flips: ARM SVE2 wins at about 2.3× serial, and the emulation barely beats scalar code. The native instruction's future is uncertain. VP2INTERSECT ships on Tiger Lake and AMD Zen 5 but is absent from Sapphire Rapids and later Xeons, and AVX10.1 excludes it ([Wikipedia: AVX-512](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AVX-512)). An AVX-512F emulation was reported faster than native on the Intel hardware tested ([Díez-Cañas 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.06342)). For heavily skewed, memory-bound cases, interleaving four binary searches to overlap cache misses ("shotgun") gives about **1.4×** and is used in CRoaring ([Lemire 2019](https://lemire.me/blog/2019/01/16/faster-intersections-between-sorted-arrays-with-shotgun/)).

On compressed data, "intersecting in the compressed domain" usually means one of two things. The first is decoding blocks fast enough that decode-then-intersect is cheap: S4-BP128-D4 decodes at about **0.7 cycles per integer**, nearly 9 billion integers per second ([Lemire et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.6399)). The second is choosing containers whose native form is already SIMD-friendly. CRoaring's SIMD paths speed up intersections **2.0–2.6×** but unions only **1.5×**. Union output is about n1+n2 and is limited by stores, and the merge networks carry fixed shuffle overhead. CRoaring deliberately keeps union at 128-bit vectors because AVX2 "gains are modest" ([Lemire et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.07821)). Lucene gets similar effects in Java by packing several integers into each long "to get SIMD-like speedups" and, since 10.3, by filtering hits with branchless vectorized code ([Lucene 10.1 javadoc](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_1_0/core/org/apache/lucene/codecs/lucene101/Lucene101PostingsFormat.html); [Lucene CHANGES](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_4_0/changes/Changes.html)).

GPUs help with throughput, not with the latency of a single conjunctive query. In 2009, GPU conjunctive intersection was only about **15% faster** than CPU even with data already on the GPU, though disjunctive queries gained 7× ([Ding et al. 2009](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/gpu.pdf)). Later designs raised throughput: 14.67× on uncompressed lists and 8.81× on compressed lists over a single CPU thread ([Ao et al. 2011](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol4/p470-ao.pdf)). Griffin's CPU–GPU scheduler was **10× faster** than an optimized CPU-only engine and cut p99.9 latency by 26.8×. It also found that more than 64% of real intersections had size ratios below 128:1, where GPU binary search loses to merge-path ([Griffin 2018](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/csjgwang/pubs/PPoPP18_Search.pdf)). Graph mining is where GPU set intersection is most active. The field moved from binary search to hashing: TRUST runs 50.1× faster on average than TriCore on a V100 ([TRUST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.08053)). The 2026 GPU sparse-retrieval work skips intersection altogether. GPUSparse uses batched scatter-add over warp-aligned posting lists and reports 1.27 ms per SPLADE query on an H100, against 298 ms for Pyserini on CPU ([GPUSparse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26441)). It is an unreplicated single-author preprint, and its CPU baseline appears to be single-threaded.

Specialized hardware is still mostly simulated or aimed at other workloads. The IIU accelerator works directly on compressed indexes. It reports **13.8× lower latency** and 5.4× higher throughput than Lucene, but only in cycle-level simulation ([IIU, ASPLOS 2020](https://asplos.hosting2.acm.org/wp/wp-content/uploads/2020/abstracts/paper_12_5.html)). SISA's set-centric processing-in-memory ISA processes dense sets as bitvectors with in-DRAM bulk bitwise operations. It handles sparse sets as sorted arrays, choosing merge or galloping at runtime, which is the same hybrid layout seen elsewhere ([SISA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07582)). On real UPMEM hardware in 2025, a GPU "consistently outperforms" both CPU and PIM on static triangle counting ([arXiv 2505.04269](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04269)). Near-memory work is moving toward learned-sparse ANN search, where memory bandwidth dominates; SpANNS reports 15.2–21.6× over CPU ([SpANNS 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03229)). I found no FPGA accelerator built specifically for posting-list intersection. For multicore, value-range (quantile) partitioning scales almost linearly ([Tsirogiannis et al. 2009](https://vldb.org/pvldb/vol2/vldb09-pvldb37.pdf)), but production engines mostly parallelize across queries and shards. A tuned multicore SIMD CPU therefore remains the default for latency-bound conjunctive queries. Note that the published crossover thresholds (50:1, 1,000:1, 128:1) were measured on Sandy Bridge and Kepler-era hardware. No unified benchmark on Zen 5 or Sapphire Rapids exists.

## Production engines converged on block-max windows over bitsets

### Lucene's four-year rewrite made windowed MaxScore the default

Ranked retrieval adds score-based pruning on top of set operations. MaxScore splits terms into "essential" and "non-essential" groups. WAND re-sorts cursors to find a pivot. Block-Max WAND and its variable-block variant VBMW add per-block score maxima, and VBMW was "roughly 2×" faster than prior methods ([VBMW, SIGIR 2017](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/344721)). In the ECIR 2019 PISA study, **VBMW was fastest overall**, but MaxScore won for k > 100 and for long queries ([Mallia et al. 2019](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/daat-ecir19.pdf)).

Lucene's history shows where production ended up. It adopted block-max WAND in 2019, which made disjunctions up to 15× faster ([Elastic 2019](https://www.elastic.co/blog/faster-retrieval-of-top-hits-in-elasticsearch-with-block-max-wand)). It switched to **block-max MaxScore in July 2022** because of its lower per-hit overhead ([Elastic Search Labs](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/more-skipping-with-bm-maxscore)). In 2023 it combined MaxScore with Doug Cutting's 1997 bitset-window ("BS1") scorer. Before that change, pruning on 24-term high-frequency disjunctions had been **2.5× slower** than exhaustive evaluation, and some learned-sparse ELSER queries ran 5× slower under WAND ([Elastic 2023](https://www.elastic.co/blog/speedups-top-k-queries-high-frequency-terms)). Lucene 9.9 turned disjunctions into conjunctions once the score threshold requires several clauses to match (+11% on OrHighHigh) ([Elastic Search Labs](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/more-skipping-with-bm-maxscore)). Lucene 9.12 inlined two-level skip data, giving +33% on CountAndHighMed ([Grand, Apr 2025](https://jpountz.github.io/2025/04/12/why-is-Tantivy-faster-than-Lucene.html)). Lucene 10.2 added bitset-encoded dense blocks and `intoBitSet` loading. Lucene 10.3 evaluated scoring windows term-at-a-time and added batch `nextDocsAndScores` ([Lucene CHANGES](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_4_0/changes/Changes.html)). Its vectorized block-max AND scores a batch from the lead clause first, prunes it with a branchless SIMD filter, and only then checks the other clauses, for about **40%** on AndHighHigh ([Grand, Aug 2025](https://jpountz.github.io/2025/08/28/compiled-vs-vectorized-search-engine-edition.html)). The disjunctive counterpart caps inner windows at 4,096 docs, each with a bitset and a score array ([Grand, Oct 2025](https://jpountz.github.io/2025/10/11/vectorized-evaluation-of-disjunctive-queries.html)). Elastic reports almost **60% higher query throughput during 2025** on Lucene's nightly benchmarks, excluding hardware changes ([Elastic, Jan 2026](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/apache-lucene-wrapped-2025)). By June 2026, Lucene's stated goal was to "skip nearly as effectively as block-max WAND while evaluating hits nearly as efficiently as exhaustive evaluation" ([Grand, Jun 2026](https://jpountz.github.io/2026/06/04/an-ode-to-self-optimizing-query-plans.html)).

As a result, the old split between document-at-a-time (DAAT) and term-at-a-time (TAAT) processing has blurred. Engines use DAAT, guided by skip and block-max metadata, to pick which windows to visit. Inside each window they accumulate TAAT-style into a bitset and a score array. Lucene's `BooleanScorer` uses 4,096-doc windows (`SHIFT = 12`) ([BooleanScorer.java](https://github.com/apache/lucene/blob/main/lucene/core/src/java/org/apache/lucene/search/BooleanScorer.java)). Tantivy's `BufferedUnionScorer` uses a 64×64 = 4,096-doc horizon ([tantivy buffered_union.rs](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/tantivy/blob/main/src/query/union/buffered_union.rs)). iresearch calls its approach "block-at-a-time" ([search-benchmark-game](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/search-benchmark-game)). Vespa takes another route. It offers an exact `wand` and an approximate `weakAnd`, and in one MS MARCO example WAND fully ranked 196,900 documents instead of 7.93M and returned the same results ([Vespa docs](https://docs.vespa.ai/en/ranking/wand.html)). Score-at-a-time processing over impact-ordered indexes (JASS) remains the natural choice under hard latency budgets. Anytime ranking over topically clustered, BP-reordered document-ordered indexes beats it across most of the likely response-time range while still supporting Boolean conjunction ([Mackenzie et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08976)).

| TOP_1000 union (median) | 1,077 | 1,042 | 681 | **313** | 730 |
| COUNT union | 936 | 364 | 368 | 3,002 | **303** |
| TOP_100_COUNT union | 3,253 | 5,206 | 4,658 | 2,948 | **1,480** |

Three patterns stand out. BP reordering alone gives Lucene another **20–30%**. PISA's BMW with directly stored impacts still has the best median for deep top-k unions, but it lacks bitset/popcount paths for counting, so its COUNT unions are about 10× slower than iresearch's. iresearch's block-at-a-time design is best for exhaustive scoring plus counting. Grand attributes Lucene's lead on long queries to MaxScore beating BMW, and fast counting to dense bitset postings that make counting cost "less than one instruction per match" ([Grand, May 2025](https://jpountz.github.io/2025/05/12/analysis-of-Search-Benchmark-the-Game.html)).

### Learned sparse weights forced docID-range pruning

Learned sparse retrieval (SPLADE, uniCOIL, DeepImpact) broke the assumptions behind per-term upper bounds. SPLADEv2 queries average about **25 unique terms**, and their "wacky weights" are not Zipfian. On MS MARCO, Lucene BMW took **2,140 ms** per SPLADEv2 query and PISA MaxScore 220 ms. PISA's WAND and BMW (619 and 681 ms) were slower than exhaustive OR (553 ms) ([Mackenzie, Trotman & Lin 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.11540v2)). The fixes stopped using per-term list bounds. Postings clipping splits lists by impact and gives up to 9.6× with no effectiveness loss ([Mackenzie et al., EMNLP Findings 2022](https://preview.aclanthology.org/new-sigs/2022.findings-emnlp.205/)). Block-Max Pruning (BMP, SIGIR 2024) splits the docID space into small blocks, sums query-weighted block maxima with SIMD, and scores the surviving blocks with a forward index. It cut rank-safe SPLADE retrieval from 120.6 ms (PISA MaxScore) to about **10.5 ms** ([BMP](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.01117v1)). Superblock pruning (SP, SIGIR 2025) adds a second level of the hierarchy and reaches **2.15 ms** rank-safe and 0.629 ms at 99% recall, against 2.70 and 1.44 ms for BMP on the same hardware ([SP](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17045v1)). Lightweight Superblock Pruning (LSP, SIGIR 2026) reports 1.8–17× over SP ([LSP](https://sites.cs.ucsb.edu/~tyang/papers/2026SIGIR-LSP.pdf)). Seismic statically prunes and clusters each list and skips clusters by summary vectors, answering in **187 µs** at 90% accuracy versus about 100 ms for exact PISA ([Seismic](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18812v1)). SeismicWave reaches 99% accuracy in 409 µs ([SeismicWave](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04443v1)). Seismic is the one that reached production. OpenSearch 3.3 runs it over 1.29B documents at 11.77 ms average with 90.2% recall@10, against 125 ms for standard neural sparse search, at a cost of about 1 GB of memory per million documents ([OpenSearch](https://opensearch.org/?p=7662)). Elasticsearch instead relies on query-time token pruning plus Lucene's MaxScore machinery ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/es/search-labs/blog/elasticsearch-sparse-vector-query)). Rank-safe superblock methods lead at 99% recall and above and at k = 1,000. Seismic is stronger at lower recall targets, and its index is about a third the size (13 GB versus up to 39 GB) ([SP](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17045v1)).

### Analytics engines fold Roaring bitmaps and now choose leapfrog adaptively

Analytics engines without ranking evaluate predicates as Roaring AND/OR. Roaring's authors found "no clear winner" between a naive in-place fold and a priority queue for 200-way unions. They suggested the naive fold, which needs O(B) memory instead of the heap's O(BN), as a good default ([Lemire et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.06549)). RoaringBitmap's `FastAggregation.or` calls `naive_or` ([FastAggregation.java](https://github.com/RoaringBitmap/RoaringBitmap/blob/master/roaringbitmap/src/main/java/org/roaringbitmap/FastAggregation.java)). Druid and Pinot both fold this way ([Druid](https://github.com/apache/druid/blob/master/processing/src/main/java/org/apache/druid/collections/bitmap/RoaringBitmapFactory.java); [Pinot OrDocIdSet](https://github.com/apache/pinot/blob/master/pinot-core/src/main/java/org/apache/pinot/core/operator/docidsets/OrDocIdSet.java)). Search engines switch from heap merging to bitsets at explicit thresholds. Lucene rewrites multi-term queries with more than 16 terms into a doc-ID set. `DocIdSetBuilder` upgrades to a bitset above 1/128 density. The query cache stores filters as Roaring below 1% density and as a `FixedBitSet` above it ([LRUQueryCache](https://github.com/apache/lucene/blob/main/lucene/core/src/java/org/apache/lucene/search/LRUQueryCache.java); [DocIdSetBuilder](https://github.com/apache/lucene/blob/main/lucene/core/src/java/org/apache/lucene/util/DocIdSetBuilder.java)). The newest data point comes from the analytics side. ClickHouse 26.8 made lazy cursor-based posting decoding the default. ClickHouse 26.10 added an `auto` intersection mode that "uses leapfrog only when the sparsest list can skip whole packed blocks of the densest one" and falls back to brute-force bitmap AND otherwise ([ClickHouse settings](https://clickhouse.com/docs/reference/settings/session-settings/text-index)). Analytics engines are thus adopting search-engine-style skipping, while search engines adopt analytics-style bitmaps.

## Compact tries and learned codecs buy speed, rarely space

Succinct primitives underpin PEF, but succinct structures used directly cost space on real postings. On URL-sorted Gov2, a `sd_vector` (an EF-based rank/select bitvector) costs 8.45 bits per integer, against 3.64 for Elias-δ gaps ([Arroyuelo & Castillo 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.00946)). The more ambitious compact structures each won in a specific niche. Wavelet-tree **dual-sorted inverted lists** were the fastest option for top-20 conjunctive queries in 2012-era comparisons, using about half the space of separate docID-sorted and frequency-sorted indexes ([Konow & Navarro 2012](https://users.dcc.uchile.cl/~gnavarro/ps/spire12.5.pdf)). Word-based self-indexes add only about 3% over the compressed text but are "orders of magnitude slower" than inverted indexes ([Konow & Navarro 2012](https://users.dcc.uchile.cl/~gnavarro/ps/spire12.5.pdf)). **Inverted treaps** store docIDs as BST keys and weights as heap priorities, which turns the heap order into a hierarchical block-max. Under tf-idf they ran ranked unions **10× (k = 10) to 3× (k = 1,000)** faster than Block-Max at about the same space. Under quantized BM25, their index grew to 35–40% larger than Block-Max and their ranked-intersection edge disappeared by k = 1,000 ([Konow et al., TOIS 2017](https://users.dcc.uchile.cl/~gnavarro/ps/tois16.pdf)). Those baselines date from 2013–2014. Nobody has re-evaluated treaps against VBMW or Lucene's current scorers, so their margins today are unknown.

The strongest recent result in compressed space is Arroyuelo and Castillo's compact binary tries (arXiv 2022; CPM 2023). They run Trabb-Pardo's classic trie intersection on level-wise bitvector tries, which achieves **O(kδ lg(u/δ))** adaptive time within trie-entropy space ([CPM 2023](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.CPM.2023.1)). A later ACM version reportedly matches Barbay–Kenyon's optimal bound in the pointer-machine model ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3812648)). Its venue and year are unconfirmed because the page was inaccessible. Run-length/gap hybrids for reordered collections report 10–30% less docID space and faster AND, WAND and OR ([Arroyuelo et al., IPM 2018](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/handle/2250/169432)); this figure comes from the abstract only. The same toolbox scales up to multiway joins. The Ring answers worst-case-optimal graph joins using 8% extra space over the raw data, running 2–6× faster than conventional RDF stores ([Arroyuelo et al., TODS 2024](https://users.dcc.uchile.cl/~gnavarro/ps/tods24.pdf)). Compressed-quadtree qdags extend this to unions and negations in less than raw space ([TODS 2022](https://users.dcc.uchile.cl/~gnavarro/abstracts/tods22.html)).

Learned methods progressed in stages. The first was a 2018 storage estimate with no trained model or timings ([Oosterhuis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.06678)). Next came learned rank/select with piecewise-linear models (LA-vector), which a 2024 vision paper admits "cannot show dominant performance in practice" against OptPFor and BIC ([Liu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10770)). LeCo (SIGMOD 2024) is a general learned compressor evaluated on columnar and key-value data ([LeCo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15374)). The current state is **LICO (SIGMOD 2026)**, the first learned codec built specifically for inverted indexes. It combines error-bounded piecewise-linear models with residuals and ships AVX-512 intersection and bitonic-merge union operators that run directly on the compressed form ([Zhu et al. 2026](https://upenn.ferric.systems/dbscholar/sigmod_papers/2026/10.1145_3802079.pdf)).

|---|---|---|---|
| Compact binary tries (2022/23) | AND 1.55–3.32× faster than PEF; 1.07–1.91× faster than Roaring | 1.15–1.72× PEF; 0.48–1.01× Roaring | URL-sorted docIDs, lists ≥ 4,096; on CC-News only about 1.07× faster than Roaring ([arXiv 2212.00946](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.00946)) |

The win conditions are consistent across these papers: clustered (reordered) docIDs, long lists, and for ranked structures small k and tf-idf-like weights. LICO's gains are partly a hardware story, since it pairs AVX-512 VP2INTERSECT kernels against baselines that are scalar or only partly SIMD, including PEF. Its learned component contributes mainly skip-free pruning and fast linear decoding, not better compression. LICO++ also degrades on queries with five or more terms, because its larger error bound weakens NextGEQ pruning. No learned method supports ranked top-k operations, none has been tested on learned-sparse indexes, and LICO has not been independently reproduced. Learned Bloom filters in 2024–2025 target LSM key-value stores, not posting lists ([arXiv 2502.03696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03696)).

## Conclusion

The fast designs share one mechanism. Each partitions the docID universe hierarchically, discards ranges that are provably empty or provably uncompetitive, and does dense, word-parallel work inside the ranges that survive. Roaring containers, empty trie subtrees, wavelet-tree interval pruning, treap heap bounds, BMP's superblocks, Lucene's 4,096-doc windows and qdag joins are all versions of this idea. In that framing, the comparison-based intersection algorithm (galloping, merge, VP2INTERSECT) has become an inner-loop detail tuned by size ratio. The outer loop, deciding which universe ranges to touch and in what layout, decides performance. This explains why docID reordering keeps paying off across every family: it creates the clustered ranges that the outer loop exploits. The 2025 trie-measure work, which re-encodes the universe to shrink tries by up to 27% on non-web set collections ([Alanko et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10703)), suggests that layout-level optimization still has room to grow.

The main uncertainty is evidential, not conceptual. No single benchmark compares PEF, Roaring, tries, treaps and LICO on the same modern CPU with real query logs. Kernel crossover thresholds date from Sandy Bridge. The headline 2026 gains (LICO, GPUSparse, LSP) are unreplicated or measured against weak baselines. For a team building or tuning an engine today, the evidence supports this default: bit-packed blocks with bitset-encoded dense blocks, inlined skip data, rarest-first leapfrog, BP reordering, and windowed block-max MaxScore. Add Roaring for cached filters, use PEF only where memory is the binding constraint, and treat learned codecs and compact tries as strong candidates to benchmark on your own data, not as drop-in replacements.
